The 30-second version

A complete product, from blank repo to shipped installers.

Chess Terminal is a desktop app that turns learning chess openings into a game. It runs entirely on your computer: the Stockfish chess engine runs inside the app, progress is saved in a local database, and there's no account, no server and no tracking. I designed the product, wrote the spec and shipped it with automated tests, CI and installers for three operating systems.

  • 33openings, Beginner to Master
  • 215unit tests across 28 test files
  • ~15klines of strict TypeScript
  • 96%smaller app bundle (320 MB → 12 MB)

The problem

Opening theory is a wall of moves nobody explains.

Most ways of learning openings come down to memorising long lists of moves. You learn what to play but not why, so the moment your opponent goes off-script you're lost, and the line you drilled last week is already gone.

I wanted a trainer that would:

  • explain the idea behind every move, for both sides,
  • test me from memory and punish mistakes (no undo button, anywhere),
  • bring back what I forget at the right time,
  • turn my own blunders into puzzles,
  • and feel like a game, so I'd actually come back to it.

What I built

Six ways to train, one loop.

Learn a line with the coach, prove it in a memory test, beat the boss to unlock the next tier. Everything you get wrong comes back in review.

Coach

Play an opening against the bot. Every book move comes with an explanation and the idea behind your next move. If the bot plays something that rules your opening out, a switch alert tells you what to play instead. When the book runs out, Stockfish takes over and grades every move (!! ! ?! ? ??).

Memory test

Play the line with no hints. One wrong move ends the run and shows the right move and why. The bot learns where you fail: branches you got wrong come up three times more often.

Openings map

33 openings in five tiers. Each one goes Learn → Deviations → Traps → Plans → Boss. Beat any boss in a tier to unlock the next. Stars, XP, ranks and three daily challenges keep it feeling like a game.

Puzzles

Up to 30,000 Lichess puzzles filtered by theme or by your openings, plus puzzles mined from your own games: blunders and missed wins found by background analysis. Rated with Glicko-2.

Calculations

Drills for thinking before you move: enter a whole line on a frozen board, find the opponent's threat, list every check, capture and threat, or follow moves with the pieces hidden.

Review & stats

Missed moves and failed puzzles become spaced-repetition cards (SM-2). An opening passport shows score, accuracy trend and the exact positions where you usually leave the book.

How it's built

Pure logic in the middle, everything else at the edges.

All the chess and training rules live in src/core as small, pure, tested TypeScript functions. The React screens stay thin. The engine, the database and the content plug in around them.

The interesting parts

Problems I had to solve along the way.

01

Getting Stockfish to run inside a packaged app

Problem: packaged Electron apps load pages from file://, which can't fetch WebAssembly and is unreliable for Web Workers, and Stockfish needs both.

Fix: the main process registers a privileged app:// scheme that serves the UI and refuses any path outside its folder. I picked the single-threaded Stockfish build so it needs no SharedArrayBuffer or special headers, and a headless --smoke mode checks the engine loads in the installed app, not just in dev.

02

Shrinking the app from 320 MB to 12 MB

Problem: electron-builder copied every runtime dependency into the app archive, including the 200 MB Stockfish package with every build.

Fix: Vite already bundles everything the UI uses, so I moved all packages to dev dependencies and shipped only the two engine files the app needs. The app archive went from 320 MB to about 12 MB.

03

Importing 300 MB of Lichess puzzles without touching disk

Problem: the Lichess dump is compressed with pzstd, which puts a skippable frame before every data frame. Node's built-in zstd decoder rejects those frames and stops after the first one.

Fix: a small frame decoder that reads each frame's size from its header and decompresses frame by frame, streaming. The script keeps puzzles that match the app's openings plus an even spread across 16 rating buckets, and stops early once every bucket is full.

04

Grading moves the way a coach would

Problem: raw engine scores mean nothing to a learner, and grading book gambits at depth 12 would contradict the coach.

Fix: grades from centipawn loss (evals clamped, mates handled), accuracy from the Lichess win% formula, !! only for an "only move", and a one-line refutation such as "Now Qxb2 wins a pawn". Book moves aren't graded. Analysis and the bot's moves run in two separate workers, so a slow analysis never holds up the bot's reply.

05

Progress that can't go out of sync

Problem: unlocks, stars and XP stored as counters will eventually disagree with the actual history.

Fix: progress is calculated from the stored games and runs every time it's needed. Snapshots are saved for convenience but never treated as the truth. The Glicko-2 puzzle rating is checked against the worked example in Glickman's paper.

06

Opening content you can trust

Problem: one typo in a hand-written opening line breaks the coach in a way you might only notice mid-game.

Fix: a validator replays every line of every opening and rejects illegal or non-standard notation, missing explanations, duplicate moves, dead ends and unknown fields. It runs before every test run and in CI, and the app checks the content again at startup and skips a broken file instead of crashing.

How I worked

Spec first, small slices, every commit green.

I started by writing a detailed product and engineering spec: the features, the fixed tech stack, the folder structure and the rules of the game. Then I built it with an AI coding agent in 15 vertical slices, from scaffold to packaging, each one a working feature in its own commit.

  • Quality gate on every commit: typecheck, lint and tests had to pass before anything was committed.
  • Decision log: every judgement call is written down with its reason in DECISIONS.md.
  • CI/CD: GitHub Actions runs the quality gate on every push and builds the Windows, macOS and Linux installers on each version tag.
  • Visual checks: a script drives the built app headlessly and takes screenshots. The ones on this page are real captures.
  • Product iteration: the look went from a terminal UI, to a cosy fantasy game, to the current "Studio" game-menu style after I decided the first versions felt like dashboards.

What I learned

  • Keeping the rules pure and the UI thin made 215 tests cheap to write and the redesigns cheap to do.
  • Check things where they really run. The engine worked in dev long before it worked in the installer.
  • Writing decisions down as you go is faster than rediscovering them later.
  • A learning tool has to be fun to open, or nobody opens it twice.
