The 30-second version

A screen-time blocker where the unlock key is a chess puzzle.

MateToScroll blocks the apps you pick, Instagram for example, once your daily limit runs out. Solving chess exercises fills a minutes bank: +1:00 per solved exercise, +5:00 per beaten boss, −0:30 per miss. You spend from the bank on the lock screen to reopen a blocked app. Blocking and time-keeping run natively in a Kotlin Accessibility Service, and the chess side is a full training game with 30,000 offline puzzles, 33 opening courses and calculation drills.

  • 168 msfrom hitting the limit to the lock screen, measured on device
  • 30,000Lichess puzzles bundled offline in 8.1 MB
  • 188Jest tests passing, plus 33 Kotlin JUnit tests
  • 5 daysfirst commit to v2 release build (25 commits)

The problem

Blockers take time away. They don't give anything back.

Screen-time blockers like Opal stop you from opening an app, but the time you win back usually goes nowhere useful. MateToScroll is for people who scroll too much and would like to get better at chess: unlocking a blocked app costs time, and you earn that time by training.

It's the mobile version of my desktop trainer, Chess Terminal, and reuses its design system, opening content and calculation drills. The rules I set:

  • no account and no server: nothing leaves the phone,
  • the blocker must keep counting even when the app itself isn't running,
  • never charge you for screen time it didn't actually see,
  • and it has to pass Google Play's review of Accessibility apps.

What I built

A blocker on one side, a chess trainer on the other.

Native blocker & lock screen

A custom Kotlin Expo module runs an Accessibility Service that reads only the foreground app's package name. When a blocked app hits the limit, it opens the lock screen through a deep link; on device that took 168 ms. From there you spend 5, 10 or 15 minutes from the bank and the app reopens.

The minutes-bank economy

Earned time rolls over across days, with a 30-minute daily earning cap, a 60-second cooldown after 2 misses in a row, 2 emergency unlocks a day, and no time earned when you use a hint. The rules live in Kotlin as the source of truth and are mirrored in TypeScript for the UI, with unit tests on both sides.

Tactics on an animated board

A custom SVG board with tap and drag input: pieces slide in 200 ms, captures fade, castling moves both pieces, and promotion uses a picker, with sound and haptics when a piece lands. Puzzles are picked near your Glicko-2 rating, skipping the last 500 you saw.

A puzzle level map

10 worlds by rating band, from 600 to 2600. Each world is a winding path of 12 levels plus a boss, 130 nodes in all. A level is 5 puzzles with 1–3 stars; a boss is 10 puzzles with at most one mistake, worth +5:00.

Openings & calculation drills

33 openings in tiers from Beginner to Master, each a chain of Learn → Deviations → Traps → Plans → Boss, played against a book bot with three styles and a coach note on every move. The Calculate tab has four drills: a whole line on a frozen board, checks-captures-threats, find the move, and blindfold visualization.

Onboarding & progression

A short questionnaire leads to a projection of your phone time, with the math shown, then a plan. A privacy disclosure always comes before the Accessibility settings, and battery tips are tailored to the phone maker. Your chess level sets your starting rating; XP, ranks from Iron to Master, daily challenges and stats track progress.

How it's built

Kotlin keeps the clock. TypeScript plays the chess.

Anything that has to survive the app being killed lives in native Kotlin. The chess logic is pure TypeScript with no React imports, enforced by a lint rule. The two talk through a small Expo module of methods and events.

React Native + Expo Router

24 screens: five tabs (Home, Puzzles, Openings, Calculate, Blocker) plus quizzes, drills, levels, the lock screen, onboarding and stats.

The interesting parts

Android doesn't make blocking apps easy.

01

Never charge time the app didn't see

Problem: the Accessibility Service can be killed, the screen can turn off, or a tick can arrive late. Subtracting "now minus last event" could cost you minutes you never spent in the app.

Fix: the engine charges at most 30 s per step and nothing across a service restart; screen-off stops the clock, and a 15 s tick runs only while a blocked app is open. Counters are saved, and on device they survived a forced crash.

02

An Accessibility Service Google Play will accept

Problem: Play reviews Accessibility use and the "see all installed apps" permission closely, and a blocker needs both kinds of information.

Fix: the service listens only to window changes, can't read window content, and reads only the package name. The app list comes from a launcher-intent query instead of the all-packages permission, and a clear disclosure always comes before the system settings.

03

Smooth pieces on a mid-range phone

Problem: SVG pieces sliding across the board dropped frames.

Fix: pieces cached as GPU textures, Reanimated's synchronous UI-props flag turned on, dragging handled entirely on the UI thread, and sounds played on landing. In a release build on a Samsung A17: 0 missed vsyncs over 8 moves.

04

A 307 MB puzzle dump in an 8.1 MB file

Problem: the Lichess dump is far too big to ship, and a random sample would cluster around common ratings.

Fix: a script streams the dump, keeps popular puzzles, takes a seeded sample of 1,500 per 100-point band from 600 to 2599, and replays every line with the app's own chess core. Finding the next puzzle answered in under 50 ms on the real database in a test.

05

Same position, different move order

Problem: in opening training you can play a move that isn't the book move but reaches the same position, and rejecting it feels wrong.

Fix: a different move is accepted only if it reaches the exact book position at that move. A move order that only converges later is still rejected, and that limit is written down in the changelog.

06

Details Android and colorblind users notice

Problem: the design's angled orange button relied on a CSS-style skew, which Android ignores, and the green/orange "over the limit" chart failed a red-blind color check.

Fix: the button is drawn as an SVG parallelogram, and the chart uses a single color with a dashed limit line instead of relying on hue.

Quality

Tested in code and on a real phone.

  • 188 Jest tests in 29 files, all passing: the chess core, the economy and progression rules, screens and navigation with React Native Testing Library, and checks against the real bundled databases (all 130 map nodes get distinct puzzles, all 33 opening trees replay legally).
  • 33 Kotlin JUnit tests for the blocker engine and the economy rules.
  • Coverage gate: 90% on the pure chess core, enforced in the Jest config.
  • Strict checks: strict TypeScript, ESLint with a purity rule for the chess core, and Prettier.
  • On-device checks: every slice was verified on a USB-connected Samsung phone, with results logged in the changelog.
  • Privacy: no network calls at all. Crash reporting was left out on purpose, because it would have been the only traffic leaving the phone.
  • Not yet: no CI pipeline; release builds are signed locally with a config plugin.

How I worked

Plan in slices, check every one on a phone.

I wrote a build plan first and split it into numbered slices, each with acceptance criteria. A slice was done when it had tests, had been checked on a real phone, and had its own commit and changelog entry.

  • v1, slices 0–11: the blocker, the board and the puzzle loop.
  • v2, slices V0–V8: after v1, I wrote a second plan that redesigned it as the mobile version of Chess Terminal and replaced the unlock-per-win model with the minutes bank.
  • Decisions written down: no account, no chess engine, a rollover bank, the earn and penalty rates.
  • AI as an assistant: I used Claude Code to write much of the code. I wrote the specs, made the product and design decisions, reviewed the work and tested every slice on a device.

What I learned and what's next

What I learned

  • Time-keeping that must survive the app dying belongs in native code, with a mirror for the UI, and both sides need tests for the same rules.
  • Android's rules shape the product: Accessibility policy, background launches, and phone makers' battery killers.
  • A data pipeline that's seeded and checked by the app's own logic avoids shipping broken puzzles.
  • Small slices, each checked on a real device, kept a 5-day build under control.

What's next

  • Run the full loop on device with the Accessibility service on: blocked app, lock, spend or earn, reopen.
  • Start a Google Play closed test and record the Accessibility demo video.
  • Theme the system navigation bar.
  • The threat-finding drill and an engine-backed boss wait for a chess engine.
